Council Verdict — Dossier

5 advisors · 5 peer reviews · chairman synthesis · Agent Harness Hackathon
18.3 hours to deadline · Sun 30 Aug, 11:00 PDT
The question: Is Dossier a good project for this hackathon, and should it be built as planned? An agent that applies to jobs the way each company requires — resolving the company's board, fingerprinting the apply route, reading the real form, filling it in a browser, and stopping at a harness approval gate. ~16h of planned work against ~18h remaining, solo.

The verdict

The idea is good. Keep it. But the council's loudest recommendation — “cut to Greenhouse only” — is wrong, and following it would produce a demo where the agent never actually submits anything.

Greenhouse always hits the reCAPTCHA wall. It is the richest source of form schemas and it gives you the wall moment — but it can never be the route that completes an application. The email route is the only one where the agent genuinely finishes the job by itself. So email is not a cut candidate; it is load-bearing. Cut Workday and Ashby instead.

Where the council agreed

  1. The schedule is not a plan. Four of five said this independently. 16h against 18h with zero slack, and a cut list you consult when desperate is not the same as a decision rule.
  2. Phase 3 is a single point of failure. Playwright blocks six downstream phases against the least predictable surface there is — real-world DOMs.
  3. The pitch verb overpromises. “Applies to jobs” versus what mostly happens — “fills the form, you click submit.” This must be the headline, not a caveat.
  4. The wall-stop is the actual product thesis — and it is currently one bullet point out of eight.

Where the council clashed

PositionWhoArgument
CUT HARDContrarian, Executor, First Principles One route only. Kill Workday, Ashby, Start All. Breadth buys no judging points and spends the only scarce resource on integration risk.
EXPANDExpansionist Start All across five real companies live is the proof TrueForge does real orchestration. Without it you have one linear script.

Both are right about different criteria. Cutting protects technical excellence and presentation; expanding protects sponsor-tool use. Peer review sided against the Expansionist on timing while siding for its diagnosis.

Blind spots caught in peer review

  1. Nobody mentioned Qodo — flagged by 4 of 5 reviewers, the strongest signal in the whole council. The rules word criterion 4 as “is TrueForge central… and did Qodo review the pull requests.” That's half an axis, and the merged PR carries external latency nobody budgeted for.
  2. Nobody did the sleep arithmetic. 18.3h wall clock minus ~6h sleep minus breaks is ~11–12h effective against 16h planned. That is a 4–5 hour deficit, not a tight schedule. Cuts are mandatory, not optional.
  3. The “cut to Greenhouse” advice is self-defeating — it removes every genuine auto-submit case. Caught only in review, by comparing the recommendation against the project's own verified findings.
  4. Demoing live against real production ATS means spamming real recruiters and betting your three minutes on sites you don't control.
  5. Résumé PII inside the sandbox is its own safety surface, separate from the gate.
  6. TrueForge's own live reliability is a distinct risk from Playwright's.

The recommendation

  1. Keep two routes: Greenhouse + email. Greenhouse gives schema richness and the wall moment. Email gives the only genuine completion. Workday and Ashby become “designed for” in the README. Keep Start All — it costs little once sessions are per-job, and it is the orchestration proof.
  2. Rename the promise. Not “applies to jobs.” Something like “does everything up to the click the platforms reserve for a human.” Put the tally on the dashboard — 2 submitted · 3 handed off · walls named — the thesis in one glance.
  3. Replace the cut list with a clock trigger. A named time, written down, after which you stop and ship what works.
  4. Record the demo incrementally as each phase lands, not at hour 17.

The one thing to do first

Write this at the top of the plan, with the real time in it:

“If inspect_form and fill_form are not working on one real form by 23:30 PDT tonight, abandon browser automation entirely and ship Greenhouse-schema + email-submit.”

Four of five advisors identified schedule collapse as the dominant failure mode. A trigger with a number on it is the only thing that converts that warning into a decision you'll actually make while tired.

Full advisor responses

The Contrarian — “that's not a build plan, that's a hope schedule”

Fatal flaw isn't in the tech — it's in what “done” means. Verification went to the hard parts and none to what judges score: does an 18-hour solo build with zero slack produce something that runs live in 3 minutes without narrating over a crash. Phase 3 is the most fragile category of software to write; if it slips 90 minutes, sandbox, gate, dashboard, README, Qodo review and video get squeezed simultaneously. No fallback, no cut line. Second: your own research proves the headline feature is mostly theater. Ask now, not at hour 16: what one thing, if it breaks, kills the demo — and do you have a recorded fallback clip already?

The First Principles Thinker — “you're optimizing the wrong variable”

“Win the hackathon” decomposes into maximizing score across six axes in ~14 effective hours, not building the product you'd ship in three months. Full autonomous ATS submission is structurally impossible — that's proof the kill feature doesn't exist. What's novel isn't reaching five platforms; it's that the harness makes a judgment call, asks a human what it can't know, refuses to fabricate a claim, and stops before an irreversible act. Demonstrable with one reliable route plus one wall example. Breadth isn't buying judging points.

The Expansionist — “the wall-stop is the whole pitch, make it unmissable”

Judges aren't grading a job-application tool, they're grading an agent harness doing real orchestration. The verified research is the asset, not the feature. The stop-and-hand-over behavior is control-and-safety AND impact AND presentation pointing at the same three seconds of footage. Give it a running tally — “3 auto-submitted, 5 handed off, walls named” — that's the thesis in one glance. Bigger swing: Start All across five real companies, triaged in parallel.

The Outsider — “that's a session count, not an architecture”

TrueForge is the hard requirement and one of six criteria, and it's the least-described part of the plan. Everything else gets verified findings and hour estimates; TrueForge gets one line. Slugify → probe → fingerprint → read schema → fill → screenshot → stop reads like a deterministic pipeline with an LLM drafting text at one step, not an agent reasoning through a harness. And the pitch verb is “applies to jobs” when the findings say it mostly can't — that gap must be the headline, not a caveat, or it plays as overpromising.

The Executor — “cut now, while it's cheap”

16h against 18h including sleep is a rounding error from failure. Phase 3 gates everything, and browser automation against real DOMs always runs long. You've designed four products; judges watch one run. Cut to one route, kill Start All, move Workday and Ashby to “designed for” in the README. And write the demo script and record a placeholder walkthrough NOW, before Phase 3 lands, so video isn't a blind estimate at hour 17 with a tired brain.

Peer review highlights

Which response was strongest, and what everyone missed

Three of five reviewers picked the Outsider — the only advisor who interrogated the mandatory TrueForge requirement rather than repackaging the project's own research. “A deterministic pipeline wearing an agent costume” risks a full axis outright.

One picked the Expansionist, on the grounds it was the only one to follow the verified finding to its conclusion: if no ATS allows unauthenticated submission, the wall-stop isn't a fallback, it's the only thing that ever happens on an ATS route.

Biggest blind spot, four of five votes: the Expansionist — right diagnosis, wrong prescription, arguing for more surface area on a build with zero slack and never once engaging the hour budget.

One picked the Executor — the only advisor whose advice was an executable checklist rather than a stance, and the only one that avoided claiming Greenhouse could auto-submit.

The sharpest catch came from the third reviewer: the First Principles recommendation is internally contradictory. Greenhouse always hits the wall, so “Greenhouse only, cut email and bespoke” leaves zero genuine auto-submit cases — the opposite of what it intended.
